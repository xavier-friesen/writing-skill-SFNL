# Shapes

The **shape** is the order a piece runs in. It is not the style and not the pyramid.

- The **pyramid** never varies: one answer, groups that support it, evidence under those. It is the
  logic of the piece.
- The **style** is texture: how a heading is worded, how a number is introduced, how loud the
  verdict is. `STYLES.md`.
- The **shape** is sequence: what comes after what, and why the reader accepts that order.

One apex can be delivered in a dozen orders, and the wrong one makes a true argument unreadable.
Pick the shape from the material, not from the style — a preset may suggest one, but a methodology
written in the case-and-lesson shape reads as a case study with no cases.

## The catalogue

Seventeen shapes in five families. Each entry: what it is, when it fits, and how it fails.

### Answer first — the reader must decide or act

| Shape | Order | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `scqa` | situatie → complicatie → vraag → antwoord | anything where the reader needs to feel the problem before the answer lands; Minto's own opening | the situation is common knowledge — then it is throat-clearing |
| `bluf` | conclusion, then detail in descending importance | mail, status note, press text, anything read half-way and abandoned | the argument is cumulative: the last paragraph carries the weakest point, so nothing may depend on it |
| `opties` | question → options with their trade-offs → recommendation | a decision with real alternatives; a board or steering document | the options are not real, and the piece performs a choice that was already made |
| `vraag-analyse-antwoord` | question → method and analysis → answer | a methodology, a calculation, an assessment, a due diligence | the analysis is long and the reader wanted the number: put the answer up front and keep the method as support |

### Evidence-driven — the reader must be convinced

| Shape | Order | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `knelpunt-casus-les` | bottleneck → cases → lesson → recommendation | the SFNL chapter form: a claim carried by practice | there are fewer than two cases, or the cases do not differ in what they prove |
| `claim-bewijs-tegenwerping` | claim → evidence → strongest objection → response | a contested position, a rebuttal, a tender answer under scrutiny | the objection is a straw man; a weak counter-argument damages more than none |
| `vergelijking` | criterion by criterion, or object by object | two or more approaches, funders, instruments weighed side by side | the criteria are chosen after the winner is; state the criteria before the comparison |
| `oorzaak-gevolg` | cause → effect, or effect → back to cause | explaining how something came about, or what a measure sets in motion | the material only shows correlation. Then this shape is a lie, whatever the wording |
| `indeling` | the field divided into categories | mapping a landscape: instruments, players, funding forms | the categories overlap or leave a gap — the reader will find it |
| `werking` | name it → how the money or the work flows → what it makes people do | a mechanism, a construction, a process; the house's strongest move | the reader needed to know *whether* it works, not how |

### Narrative — the reader must see it

| Shape | Order | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `chronologisch` | in time order | a project history, a reconstruction, a timeline with a turning point | nothing turns: then it is a log, and the reader stops at the third date |
| `situatie-actie-resultaat` | situation → what we did → what it produced | a case description, a project page, a reference | the result is thin — the shape promises a payoff and must have one |
| `zandloper` | conclusion up front → narrative middle → back to meaning | a case that must be both scannable and told: reader gets the outcome, then the story | the middle has no scene in it; then it is a bluf with a long tail |
| `scene-nutgraf` | one concrete scene → the paragraph saying why this matters → the piece | a feature, a portrait, a newsletter, a project page that has to be felt | the scene is decorative and the nut graf could have opened the piece by itself |

### Scholarly — the reader will check

| Shape | Order | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `imrad` | introduction → method → results → discussion | an article, a working paper, an evaluation held to academic standards | interpretation leaks into the results, or the method is written after the conclusion was chosen |
| `cars` | territory → niche → occupation | the introduction *inside* `imrad`, and any piece that must justify why it exists | there is no real gap; then the move-2 sentence turns into a rhetorical gesture |

### State of play — the reader must keep track

| Shape | Order | Fits | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| `stand-opvallend-nu` | where it stands → what stands out → what happens next | a progress note, a steering document, a quarterly update | everything is reported at equal weight: the middle step is the one that earns the note |

## Composing shapes across sections

A long piece is not one shape. It is one apex with a **top-level shape**, and sections that each run
their own — exactly what the 2025 report does, verified in `dossier/test-vormen.md`: the chapter is
`knelpunt-casus-les`, each case inside it is `situatie-actie-resultaat` with a `werking` passage,
and each recommendation page is its own small `knelpunt → voorstel → werking → wat het doorbreekt`.

Six rules keep that from becoming a patchwork.

1. **One apex, one top-level shape.** The shape of the whole piece is the order of its sections and
   nothing else.
2. **A section's shape serves its slot.** Ask what this section does for the apex — prove, explain,
   compare, tell — and pick the shape that does that job. A section whose shape does not match its
   job is the commonest structural error, and it reads as drift, not as variety.
3. **One shape per section.** Switching inside a section loses the reader mid-paragraph. If a
   section wants two shapes, it is two sections.
4. **Parallel sections take the same shape.** Three cases proving the same kind of thing run
   identically. Varying them for freshness reads as sloppiness, and it costs the comparison.
5. **Two levels, no more.** Piece and section. When a subsection needs its own declared shape, the
   section should have been split.
6. **Signal every switch in one sentence at the seam.** The reader accepts a change of order if the
   text says what the next part does. Unannounced switches are where readers put a piece down.

## Choosing

Start from the job, not the genre:

| The reader must… | Start from |
|---|---|
| decide between real alternatives | `opties` |
| act on a conclusion, fast | `bluf` |
| accept a claim that is contested | `claim-bewijs-tegenwerping` |
| understand how something works | `werking` |
| believe it works in practice | `knelpunt-casus-les` or `situatie-actie-resultaat` |
| judge whether the method is sound | `vraag-analyse-antwoord` |
| see the field | `indeling` or `vergelijking` |
| know what happened | `chronologisch` or `zandloper` |
| feel it | `scene-nutgraf` |
| stay informed | `stand-opvallend-nu` |
| check the work | `imrad`, with `cars` in its introduction |

Propose the shape at intake, in the same line as the style, and put it in the sidecar next to the
apex. **None of the seventeen fitting is a normal outcome, not a failure**: describe the order you are
using in one sentence, say why the material demands it, and hold it as if it were on the list.
